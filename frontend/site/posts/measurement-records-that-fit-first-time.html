Measurements · 6 min read

Measurement records that actually fit the first time

The numbers are the easy part. What separates a first-fit garment from a second fitting is everything written in the margin.

Every boutique records measurements. Very few record enough of them to make a garment fit without a fitting — not because the measuring is bad, but because the notebook holds numbers and the knowledge that turns numbers into a garment stays in the master's head.

That works until the master is on leave, or the client returns after eighteen months, or the person who took the original measurements has moved on. Then the numbers are all that is left, and they turn out to have been the smaller half of the record.

The numbers are necessary and insufficient

A bust measurement of 36 inches is a fact. What it does not tell the next person is whether that was taken over a padded blouse, whether the client prefers a close fit or breathing room, whether one shoulder sits lower than the other, or whether the last garment made from this number came back for alteration.

All four of those change the garment. None of them are numbers.

What to record alongside the measurements

Who took them, and when

The single most useful field, and the most commonly missing. Measurements taken by different people are not interchangeable — two masters can measure the same client and legitimately differ by half an inch depending on tension and landmarks. Knowing whose numbers these are tells the cutter how to read them.

The date matters for the obvious reason. A record with no date is a record you cannot trust and cannot discard.

What she was wearing

Measurements taken over a padded blouse and measurements taken over a thin kurta describe different bodies. One line — "over padded blouse" — prevents a garment that is inexplicably tight.

Ease preference, in her words

"Likes it snug at the waist, loose at the arm" is worth more than three extra measurements. It is also the thing most likely to be remembered by the person who took it and nobody else.

Posture and asymmetry notes

Almost nobody is symmetrical. A dropped shoulder, a forward neck, a fuller left side — experienced cutters compensate automatically and never write it down. Write it down. It is the difference between a garment that hangs correctly and one that needs a fitting to discover the obvious.

What was made from these numbers

Link the measurement set to the garments produced from it. When a client says "like the last one but looser", the last one is a specific record with specific numbers and a specific outcome.

What came back, and why

If a garment needed alteration, record what was altered against the measurement set that produced it. This is the feedback loop, and it is the part almost everyone skips. Without it, the same half-inch error repeats for years.

A test for your current records. Pick a client who last ordered eighteen months ago. Hand her file to a tailor who has never met her and ask whether they could cut her garment without calling anyone.

If the answer is no, the gap between what is written and what is known is the gap that produces second fittings.

Why a history beats a current set

Most measurement books hold the latest numbers and overwrite the old ones. That throws away the most useful signal you have: change over time.

A client whose measurements have moved steadily over three years is telling you something about how to cut her next garment. A client whose numbers jumped once and returned is telling you that one of those sets was taken badly. You cannot see either if each new measurement replaces the last.

Keep the sets. Date them. Never overwrite.

The bridal exception

Bridal work breaks the normal rules because the body is often deliberately changing between order and delivery, and the date cannot move. Two habits help: measure again closer to delivery rather than trusting the order-day set, and record the intended date of the final fitting on the order itself, so it is a scheduled commitment rather than something remembered in the last fortnight.

Where this ends up

None of this needs software. A well-designed page in a register does all of it, and plenty of excellent boutiques run exactly that way.

What a system adds is that the fields are there whether or not the person is in a hurry, the history is not overwritten by accident, and the record follows the client rather than the notebook — which matters most on the day the notebook is at someone's home and the client is at your counter.
