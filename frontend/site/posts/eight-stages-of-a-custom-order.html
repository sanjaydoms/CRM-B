The eight stages every custom tailoring order goes through

Whether you have named them or not, your floor already runs on stages. Naming them is what makes a delay visible while there is still time to do something about it.

A retail sale has two states: unsold, then sold. A made-to-order garment has neither for most of its life. It exists as an agreement, then as fabric, then as pieces, then as something that needs pressing — and during all of that, "where is it?" is a question with a real answer that nobody has written down.

Here are the eight stages that cover almost every made-to-order boutique, what each one is actually for, and where the time really goes.

1. Received

The client, the garment and the measurements are captured. This is the cheapest place in the entire process to make a mistake, and the most expensive place to make one that goes unnoticed — a measurement taken carelessly here becomes an alteration in week four, after fabric has been cut.

Owned by: whoever takes measurements. If that is a different person each time, this is the first thing to fix.

2. Confirmed

Design agreed, costing set, advance recorded. The value of a distinct confirmation stage is that it draws a line: before it, a cancellation costs a conversation; after it, it costs fabric. Boutiques that skip this stage tend to start cutting on verbal agreement and discover the disagreement later.

3. Stylist review

Design references and fabric checked together before anything is committed. The part worth being strict about is recording where the reference came from — your own catalogue, a previous garment of hers, a customer sketch, a screenshot from Pinterest. "The one she showed me on her phone" is not a specification, and three weeks later nobody will be able to reconstruct it.

4. Design and creation

The longest stage, and the one that hides the most. Inside it sit pattern drafting, cutting, embroidery and stitching — four different people, often four different days, and on embroidered pieces one supplier who is not in your building.

This is where nearly every late delivery comes from, and almost never for the reason people assume. It is rarely that stitching is slow. It is usually that the piece sat waiting for the embroidery run, or waiting to be cut because the pattern master had four other jobs. If you only track one thing, track the sub-steps here.

If every order in your workshop is waiting at the same sub-step, you do not have a speed problem. You have a queue, and it is telling you where to hire.

5. Quality check

The finished garment is inspected against the measurement ledger it was supposed to be made from. Two things make this stage worth having as a formal step rather than a habit: it happens before the customer sees the garment, and a failure records a reason.

Those reasons are worth more than they look. After a season they answer a question most boutiques cannot: which stage is generating our rework? Alterations feel random until they are attributed, at which point they are usually concentrated in one or two causes.

6. Ready for dispatch

Pressed, packed, photographed, balance settled. Photographing here rather than at delivery is worth the ten minutes — it gives you a record of what left the building, which is occasionally the only thing that settles a dispute about condition.

7. Shipped

Courier and tracking number, or counter pickup. Small stage, but the one where customer anxiety peaks, because the garment has left your control and hers has not started.

8. Delivered

Closed, with the history kept against the client. That history is what makes her second order faster than her first: the measurements are there, the fit notes are there, and what she asked to be changed last time is there.

Not every order needs every stage

A plain blouse with no embroidery should not sit waiting at a Maggam stage that does not apply to it. The stage list belongs to the order, not to the boutique — a simple garment carries a short list, a bridal piece carries a long one. A stage list that cannot be shortened will be ignored within a month, which is worse than not having one.

What to do with this

You do not need software to start. Take the eight stages, cross out the ones you do not do, and write the name of the person who owns each remaining one. That exercise alone usually surfaces two things: a stage nobody owns, and a stage where everything waits.

Fix those two and you will have recovered more delivery time than any tool would have given you.
